PROJECT 01

Silver–Curcumin–Berberine Nanocomplex

Project Overview

Research title
Process for Producing a Silver Nanoparticle Dual Complex Conjugated with Curcumin and Berberine with Broad-Spectrum Antibacterial Efficacy
Research period
Mar – Oct 2026
Research field
Biotechnology & Biomedical Materials
My role
Co-author
Supervisor
Assoc. Prof. Nguyen Tien Dung, PhD
Link to paper
Forthcoming on wipopublish.ipvietnam.gov.vn (Barcode label: 2-2026-01087 HI)

Summary

I co-developed a process for producing silver nanoparticles conjugated with curcumin and berberine inside a protective phospholipid layer, creating a broad-spectrum antibacterial agent at a time when antibiotic resistance has become one of the world’s most serious health threats.

The World Health Organization classifies the emergence and spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria as one of the top ten global public health threats. Bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites mutate over time and adapt to the very drugs designed to kill them, making common infections harder to treat, prolonging hospital stays, raising healthcare costs and driving up mortality. From 2018 to 2023, resistance grew seriously in more than 40% of the antibiotics closely monitored by health systems, at an average rate of 5–15% per year, and in 2023, one in every six confirmed bacterial infections was caused by a drug-resistant strain.

Resistance is spreading far faster than the pharmaceutical industry can develop new classes of antibiotics, so new antibacterial strategies are urgently needed. Our invention, which spans biotechnology, biomedical materials and molecular pharmacology, combines three components in a single nanostructure: a silver nanocore conjugated with curcumin and berberine, two naturally derived compounds, and wrapped in a stable protective phospholipid bilayer. The dual nanocomplex is formed using ultrasonic dispersion and reassembly techniques.

To evaluate its antibacterial activity, we used the agar well diffusion method against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. All work was carried out in a sterile cabinet, which we first exposed to UV light for at least 15 minutes and wiped down with 70% ethanol, working close to an alcohol burner flame to keep conditions sterile. We poured melted Mueller–Hinton agar into sterile Petri dishes, let it set, and spread each bacterial strain evenly across the surface to form a uniform lawn. Using a sterile copper tube, we punched wells into the agar in a regular polygon pattern so that they were evenly spaced and their inhibition zones would not overlap, then pipetted about 2 µL of the diluted antibacterial solution into each well. The plates were refrigerated for about 8 hours to let the samples diffuse into the agar, then incubated at 30 °C for 2–3 days. Finally, we measured the diameter of the clear inhibition zone around each well to compare how strongly different samples and concentrations suppressed bacterial growth.

Because the active components are held together and protected by a phospholipid membrane, the resulting product can be freeze-dried and readily reconstituted, and it exhibits broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. The application was filed with the Intellectual Property Office of Viet Nam on 9 October 2026, and its publication is forthcoming.

CoFe2O4 Nanoparticles

Project Overview

Research title
Research and Synthesis of CoFe2O4 Materials and Their Application in Drug Delivery
Research period
Aug 1 – Aug 31, 2026
Research field
Healthcare
My role
Team Leader
Supervisor
Assoc. Prof. Nguyen Cao Khang, PhD
View presentation slides

Summary

I synthesized cobalt ferrite (CoFe2O4) magnetic nanoparticles to test whether they could carry drugs straight to where they are needed, an approach that could lower the doses and side effects of treatments such as chemotherapy.

Drugs for cancer, diabetes and heart disease are now used everywhere, but they can kill healthy cells, lower blood pressure, or be cleared from the bloodstream too quickly to work well. Nanoparticle drug delivery systems tackle this by carrying medicine to a precise target and releasing it in a controlled way. Nanoparticles, however, bring problems of their own, such as toxicity, unwanted accumulation in the body and difficult clearance, so a good carrier has to be small, uniform and easy to steer.

Our question was whether we could produce water-dispersible, uniformly sized CoFe2O4 nanoparticles with a magnetic response strong enough to be guided by an external magnetic field. We would consider the material promising if it formed a single, well-crystallized phase, kept a consistent nanoscale size, and behaved superparamagnetically with a high saturation magnetization.

Our team prepared the particles by chemical co-precipitation. We dissolved 2.91 g of cobalt salt and 5.41 g of iron(III) chloride in 200 mL of distilled water, then added a cooled solution of 4 g of NaOH in 100 mL of water drop by drop under magnetic stirring until the pH reached 9 or above, forming a dark brown precipitate, and kept stirring for another hour. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm, washed with ethanol to reduce clumping and dried at 80–100 °C for 12 hours. Finally, we ground the powder and calcined it at 600–700 °C for 2–3 hours to convert it into crystalline CoFe2O4. We then characterized the samples with X-ray diffraction (XRD), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM).

XRD showed seven sharp peaks matching CoFe2O4, confirming a single phase with good crystallinity. TEM revealed consistently sized particles with a median diameter of 14–16 nm, and VSM showed that they are superparamagnetic at room temperature, with a saturation magnetization of about 55 emu/g. These results make the particles a strong candidate for magnetically guided drug delivery. Our next steps are to attach drugs such as antibiotics to the particles and to test magnetic nanoparticles built from metals other than cobalt.

PROJECT 03

CuO Photovoltaic Materials

Project Overview

Research title
Copper Oxide Nanomaterials for Solar Energy Harvesting and Photovoltaic Applications
Research period
Jun 15 – Jul 16, 2026
Research field
Renewable Energy
My role
Team Leader
Supervisor
Assoc. Prof. Nguyen Cao Khang, PhD

Summary

I synthesized copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticles and tested them as a low-cost light absorber for solar energy, because cheap, eco-friendly photovoltaic materials could help sun-rich countries like Vietnam move toward clean electricity.

Global energy demand keeps rising, yet much of the world’s electricity still comes from fossil fuels, while hydroelectric and nuclear power face environmental, supply or safety limits. Solar energy is far more abundant: the sunlight reaching Earth each year far exceeds global energy consumption. Vietnam receives plenty of sunlight, but turning it into electricity requires light-absorbing semiconductors that are both affordable and environmentally safe.

We asked whether CuO, a p-type semiconductor with a narrow bandgap, strong visible-light absorption, low cost and good environmental compatibility, could be produced at the nanoscale with properties suited to photovoltaic use. Our objective was to synthesize and characterize CuO nanomaterials and demonstrate a practical application. Success meant particles in the nanometer range, optical properties suited to absorbing sunlight, and a working model that converts solar energy into usable energy.

Our team prepared CuO nanomaterials through a controlled chemical synthesis and analyzed their structural and optical properties. We then designed and built a simple experimental model that converts sunlight into other usable forms of energy.

We synthesized the nanoparticles by chemical precipitation, using copper(II) sulfate (CuSO4·5H2O) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) with standard laboratory equipment: beakers, a magnetic stirrer and stir bar, a balance, a pipette, a centrifuge, an oven, and a mortar and pestle. We first dissolved 0.25 g of CuSO4·5H2O and 0.4 g of NaOH in distilled water to make 0.005 M and 0.01 M solutions, respectively. While stirring the copper sulfate solution, we added the NaOH solution dropwise at 2 mL per minute and checked the pH with pH paper; once the pH reached 9 or above and the paper turned blue, precipitation was at its maximum. We collected the precipitate by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 10 minutes and heated it in an oven at 300 °C for 4 hours. The calcined solid was then crushed with a mortar and pestle, washed with 90% ethanol and dried in an oven at 100 °C for 20 minutes. Finally, we weighed the product to calculate the yield of the process.

Under SEM, the synthesized CuO appeared as dense, granular nanoparticles about 30 nm in size, confirming the successful fabrication of a nanoscale semiconductor, and showed favorable optical properties for photovoltaic applications. Our prototype demonstrated that sunlight can serve as a primary energy source for generating electricity and other energy conversions. Together, these results support CuO as a promising material for clean, low-cost solar technologies, especially in countries with abundant sunlight such as Vietnam.

PROJECT 04

SafeDrive+

Project Overview

Research title
SafeDrive+: A Smart Blood Alcohol Monitoring System Warning System for Drivers
Research period
Feb 2026 – Present
Research field
IoT in Business
My role
Developer
Supervisor
Assoc. Prof. Nguyen Cao Khang, PhD
View presentation slides

Summary

I am developing a mobile app that estimates a driver’s blood alcohol concentration and warns them before they get behind the wheel, because drunk driving is a major cause of dangerous road accidents.

Blood alcohol concentration (BAC) measures how much ethanol is in the blood after drinking. Ethanol is absorbed through the stomach and small intestine and broken down by the liver at a rate that differs from person to person. When intake outpaces the liver, BAC rises and impairs judgment, reflexes and control; at high levels it can even cause loss of consciousness, which makes driving extremely dangerous. Yet most people have no convenient way to check themselves, and tools that rely only on manually entered data are less reliable.

SafeDrive+ explores whether a smartphone’s own sensors, combined with an established BAC formula, can give drivers a convenient and trustworthy picture of their condition. The goal is a user-friendly system that helps people check themselves before driving, reducing traffic accidents and raising public awareness. The system shows promise if it can collect real sensor data, calculate BAC reliably and turn the result into clear, timely warnings.

So far, the app, built on Thunkable/Flutter, reads acceleration data from the phone and flags patterns typical of impaired driving, such as sudden acceleration, harsh braking and failing to keep a straight line. It calculates BAC using the Widmark formula with experimental correction factors, displays the result with color-coded alerts (green for safe, yellow for caution, red for over the limit), predicts how long the body needs to clear the alcohol, and keeps a history of measurements so users can track their drinking habits. Combining real phone measurements with the algorithm makes the estimate more reliable than manual input alone.

04.1 // Dặm Xanh

damxanh.top

I co-founded Dặm Xanh out of a simple belief: healthy eating should not have to come at the expense of convenience, especially for people whose demanding routines leave them little time to care for their nutrition. I wanted to turn that belief into something tangible, so I led a team of 16 to develop the “Healthy Flight Kit,” a meal-box concept featuring Vietnamese ingredients and regional cuisines, with carefully calculated calories and portions tailored to the needs of pilots, flight attendants, and other active professionals.

Beyond providing convenient, nutritious meals, I hoped the project could help people better understand the connection between food and health. We organized a public talkshow on the gut microbiome featuring Prof. Dr. Nguyễn Trọng Hưng from the National Institute of Nutrition, bringing together 40 participants to explore how everyday dietary choices affect their well-being. Alongside developing our products and educational activities, we allocated 80% of project proceeds to fund free meals for patients through the charity kitchen at Da Nang Oncology Hospital. From working out the nutritional details of a meal box to figuring out how our proceeds could help put meals on patients’ tables, Dặm Xanh gave me the opportunity to connect my interest in nutrition with the practical work of building a project from the ground up.

04.4 // Chess and Problem Solving

Visit Ams Chess Academy on Facebook View my Chess.com profile

For me, chess has never been just a game; it is a way of training how I think. Every position is a problem without an answer key: I have to read the board, anticipate how my opponent might respond, calculate several moves ahead and commit to a plan with incomplete information, all against the clock. Reaching a 1700 Rapid rating on Chess.com taught me that progress comes less from brilliant moves than from patience, discipline and honestly reviewing the games I lose.

I find the same habits at the heart of engineering. Planning a synthesis feels a lot like planning a game: every decision, from reaction time to precursor concentration, has consequences several steps later, and each choice involves trade-offs between competing goals. Just as a lost game becomes material for analysis, a failed experiment becomes data that points toward a better design. Chess taught me to break a complicated problem into smaller ones, anticipate what could go wrong, and adapt when reality does not match my plan.

That love for the game led me to Ams Chess Academy, my school’s chess club, where I was active from September 2023 to August 2025. As part of the club’s expertise team, I helped turn chess knowledge into well-run events: I coordinated tournament preparation for the Hanoi Summer Chess Championship 2025, a city-wide competition that drew nearly 200 players, as well as the Hanoi Chess Tournament 2025, handling player coordination and competition operations.

The activity that meant the most to me was organizing chess sessions at Nguyễn Đình Chiểu School for more than 30 students with disabilities. We ran 15 simultaneous games, and I adapted the way I explained the rules and interacted with each student to suit their individual needs. That experience taught me that a good explanation has to fit the person in front of you, and that the problem-solving skills chess builds are most valuable when they are shared.

05 // Engineering Through Everyday Curiosity

Cooking

The kitchen is my favorite everyday laboratory. A recipe reads a lot like an experimental procedure: precise quantities, controlled temperatures, a careful order of steps, and a result that tells you honestly whether you got it right. When I cook, I like to change one variable at a time, such as how long something is heated or how much of an ingredient goes in, and see how it changes texture and flavor. It is the same habit I rely on in my research, and the same attention to detail I brought to calculating calories and portions for Dặm Xanh’s meal boxes.

Piano

Playing the piano taught me that precision is built, not given. A difficult passage rarely comes together on the first try, so I break it into small sections, find exactly where it falls apart, and repeat until it holds, much like debugging a process one step at a time. Music also gave me a way to share what I practice; accompanying a friend singing “Thời Thanh Xuân Sẽ Qua” in front of our class reminded me that all the hours of careful repetition are ultimately meant for other people.

Lotion Making

Making lotion turns a simple chemistry idea into something I can actually use every day. A lotion is an emulsion: oil and water that would normally separate, held together by an emulsifier. Each batch is a small experiment in balance, where adjusting the ratio of oil to water or the way the mixture is heated and stirred changes how stable, smooth and light the final product feels. Seeing that a little chemistry can turn into a useful, everyday product connects directly to what I hope to do in the future: design materials that are both practical and sustainable.
